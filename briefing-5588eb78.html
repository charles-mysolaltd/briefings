There is no clean like-for-like to check this against, because no mainstream UK retailer currently stocks a genuine grid-tied plug-in kit. Currys' entire "portable solar panel" range (Anker, Jackery, EcoFlow) is for charging power stations off-grid, camping and blackout kit rather than a wall-socket system. Those run £58.99 to £2,299, with most between £179 and £999, which at least tells us what UK shoppers already pay a high-street retailer for solar and battery gear in this general bracket. Sola's £199 to £499 sits comfortably inside that range.

The closer comparison is EcoFlow's STREAM balcony system, the one true plug-in category on the market. It is not stocked at Currys either, it is EcoFlow-direct only and was priced £449 to £749 on member pricing when we last checked on 3 June (worth re-checking, as it may have moved). Sola comes in below that across the board and does so without EcoFlow's membership-tier mechanic, where a first-time buyer sees the higher non-member price by default.

The more important gap is at the small end, not the price. Neither Currys nor EcoFlow sell anything with a flexible panel or a wattage as low as 200W. EcoFlow's smallest STREAM configuration starts at 2×400W rigid panels and Currys carries nothing grid-tied at all. A renter with a balcony cannot currently buy a light, single flexible panel from anyone visible in this market, even though that is exactly the format a balcony rail wants: a rigid glass panel is heavy and awkward to hang, a flexible one is not. Sola 200 and 400 sit in that space largely on their own.

Freight International figures are estimates 10468 and 10469, dated 4 June 2026, EXW Suzhou to St Albans, inclusive of import customs clearance and excluding duty and tax, expired 18 June 2026. Treat all of them as a starting point rather than a bookable rate.

United Global's most recent quote was USD4,300 for a 40'HQ container, FOB Shanghai to Felixstowe, plus roughly £512 in UK-side charges (terminal handling, port, documentation and customs clearance), which works out to about £7.46 a unit on the Sola 400 and £16.32 on the Sola 800 at current container fill. That is meaningfully cheaper than Freight International's £12.73 and £27.86, though the comparison is not perfectly clean: Freight International's rate is EXW Suzhou, while United Global's is FOB Shanghai, which folds in an export leg that Freight International's figure does not carry.

Rhenus's own formal quote (reference RHE20003) came in at USD5,100 for a 40HQ, the most expensive of the three, but Yasmin's more recent verbal range of USD3,850 to 4,200 is a real improvement and undercuts United Global's ocean rate. Two things stop this being a clean number yet. It is quoted for a 40FT container rather than confirmed as a 40HQ, and the extra headroom a High Cube gives matters most on the Sola 800, where the two stacked pallets already use 260cm of a 40HQ's 269cm: in a standard-height container the double stack will not fit and capacity could fall from 234 units to roughly 117, which would roughly double the per-unit freight rather than improve it. It is also ocean freight only. Rhenus's own destination-side charges, on the earlier formal quote, added around £1,426 to the container, and there is no confirmation those still apply to the new range.

For context on LCL rather than full containers, United Global's own small-batch quote (a mixed shipment of two Sola 400 and two Sola 800 pallets) works out to roughly £23 a unit on the 400 and £66 on the 800, both cheaper than Freight International's £38.52 and £76.86, though this is a proportional split of one combined quote rather than a price for each SKU on its own and should be treated as directional.

06 · Sola 200

Why there is no landed cost for this one yet.

The factory told us the MS300 microinverter will not start up on a single 200W panel input, which is why there is no Sola 200 configuration on the current quote (QO-GL-2026051901V01) or a landed cost to put next to the £199 retail price above. The nearest thing on the quote, the MS400 configuration, is already a two-panel kit at £170 EXW.

We are not convinced this is a hard technical limit rather than a conservative default the factory would rather not test for us. It is worth requesting a sample MS300 and a single panel and trying it ourselves before accepting the constraint as final, since a confirmed single-panel bill of materials is the only thing standing between the £199 price and a real cost breakdown for it.

07 · MS400, angled configuration

Withdrawn and worth revisiting.

The factory discontinued the angled configuration because the lightweight flexible 200W panel behaves like a sail in high wind when mounted at an angle, which they treat as a safety risk on a balcony rail. We are keen to get this model back on the table regardless: an angled mount generates noticeably more than the flush vertical version at UK latitudes, the same effect our own solar angle calculator models for London. Vertical capture drops off fastest exactly when the sun sits low, which is most of the year here.

One thing worth flagging to Rena directly: the factory's own public product page for the angled configuration still lists it as an active, adjustable-angle product with no discontinuation notice. It may only be withdrawn for our order or region rather than globally, which would make it worth another conversation about a compliant angled bracket instead of accepting the vertical-only line as final.

08 · Factory Open API

The paid route to our own app.

The factory offers an Open API on a paid licence, previously quoted at £2,220 one-off, that would let a Sola app pull live generation data directly rather than sending customers to the factory's own monitoring platform under our branding. It is a prerequisite for the Sola app MVP and currently sits after launch on the roadmap, but it belongs in the same supplier conversation as everything else in this document, since it is another cost sitting on the factory relationship rather than a separate line item.

09 · 3PL and logistics

Where we stand on partners.

One 3PL relationship is the preferred structure: a single warehouse and fulfilment partner rather than splitting freight, storage and last-mile across separate providers. Rhenus has been the leading candidate but has been slow to move and still has not returned a warehousing rate card, so United Global's own Wrexham facility is now being looked at as a genuine alternative rather than purely a freight option.
